# Cracked Ice editorial voice

Source corpus: the complete original versions of *Player Battles*, *Zero Bench*, and *Position Group Stacks* recovered from commit `7e3418f`. This guide governs human-facing articles. It does not govern methodology documentation or generated fact packets.

## The voice in one sentence

A fantasy-hockey obsessive who has run the numbers, has an opinion about what they mean, and would rather explain the edge over a beer than write a research paper about it.

## Core characteristics

### Personal, not institutional

Use `I` when describing an analysis, opinion, bias, surprise, or decision. Use `we` sparingly and only when genuinely referring to Cracked Ice as a product. Do not hide every judgment behind “the model” or “the data.”

Good:

- “I ran every two-team combination.”
- “My gut says Harley. The schedule says Chychrun.”
- “Not gonna lie, I’m a Canucks fan.”

Avoid:

- “Cracked Ice Analytics has determined…”
- “The more actionable insight is…”
- “The lesson is narrower…”

### Hockey-literate and specific

Use references a hockey fan would recognize, but only when they naturally fit. Player quirks, team history, draft-room arguments, lineup pain, and familiar hockey moments make the writing feel lived-in. Generic sports metaphors do not.

One good hockey reference is better than five forced jokes. Never manufacture a personal anecdote or claim an experience that did not happen.

### Confident, with visible uncertainty

Make the call when the evidence supports one. When it does not, say so plainly: “toss-up,” “I don’t know,” or “this depends on your league.” Confidence comes from showing the decision, not pretending uncertainty disappeared.

### Competitive without becoming sales copy

The originals frame schedule math as an overlooked advantage over league mates. Keep that competitive energy, but reduce the “destroy the competition” and “championship warfare” language. The reader should feel clever and equipped, not yelled at.

### Varied rhythm

Mix normal explanatory paragraphs with short sentences and occasional fragments. Use repetition when it creates emphasis, not as a template. Contractions are preferred. Rhetorical questions are useful in moderation.

## Target intensity

Aim for roughly 65% of the original articles’ intensity:

- Keep the humor, opinions, verdicts, and hockey references.
- Keep occasional irreverence.
- Reduce all-caps, repeated championship claims, combat metaphors, and insults toward other managers.
- Avoid drug or alcohol references unless Matt adds or explicitly approves them.
- Let one strong line land instead of following it with three more versions of the same line.

## Article structure

1. **Open on a recognizable decision or frustration.** Start inside the draft room, lineup screen, waiver decision, or playoff calendar—not with a definition.
2. **State the surprising result early.** Give the reader a number or decision worth continuing for.
3. **Explain enough math to earn trust.** Define assumptions once. Do not repeat the same caveat after every section.
4. **Move through concrete decisions.** Player battles, roster examples, schedule flips, and clear verdicts beat abstract principles.
5. **Acknowledge where the method stops.** Talent, role, health, eligibility, league format, and availability remain outside pure schedule math.
6. **End with a usable move.** Tell the reader what to check, compare, or change in their own league.

## AI-pattern blacklist

Rewrite these constructions when they appear:

- “This isn’t about X. It’s about Y.”
- “There is no universal…”
- “The lesson is narrower…”
- “The more actionable edge…”
- “It is important to note…”
- “In today’s fast-paced…”
- “Whether you’re a seasoned veteran or…”
- “Not merely X, but Y.”
- “Ultimately…” as a conclusion shortcut.
- Perfect three-item lists in consecutive sections.
- A disclaimer immediately after every strong sentence.
- Repeated em-dash constructions that make every paragraph sound equally polished.

Do not use fake quotations, invented reader reactions, fabricated personal stories, or unexplained certainty about injuries, roles, or availability.

## Data and editorial boundaries

- The generator owns calculations, tables, source dates, assumptions, and machine drafts.
- The editorial draft owns hooks, opinions, jokes, transitions, emphasis, and final recommendations.
- Never alter a computed number by hand. Regenerate the fact packet, then update the surrounding prose.
- State the scoring preset, lineup assumptions, and date window once near the relevant example.
- Schedule is a tiebreaker and opportunity model—not a replacement for talent, role, health, or news.
- Never call a player available without league-specific evidence.

## Final human pass

Before publication, Matt should add or approve at least two of the following:

- A genuine personal opinion.
- A hockey reference he would actually make.
- A sentence he would say out loud to a league mate.
- A disagreement with or limitation of the numbers.
- A small joke that does not depend on generic internet sarcasm.

Then read the article aloud. If every paragraph sounds equally smooth, break the rhythm. If a line could appear in any analytics company’s blog, rewrite or delete it.
